# Booth Camp

A cartoon hearing-clinic game. You are a trainee in Professor Hoot's clinic, and your first
patient is Grandpa Gus, who has been hearing "fishes" every time Doris asks him to do the dishes.
You take his history, look in his ears, explain the test, put the earphones on, run a real-style
pure-tone air-conduction test on both ears with the AudiOtron 3000, read the audiogram, crack the
case, and get a sneak peek of his first hearing aids. One visit takes about 10 to 15 minutes.

**Day 2** brings Bonnie Bunsworth, a baker whose right ear has gone "all underwater". It opens with
**Hoot's masking movie** ("The Cross-Hearing Ghost"), a 95-second cartoon in ten scenes: two snail
cochleas in one head, the skull wall that eats at least 40 dB of an earphone beep, the bone buzzer
that shakes both sides, the Ghost (the better cochlea answering for the poorer ear), the "shhh" in
the other ear, too little and too much of it, and the plateau. Space, Enter or a click work like
the dialogue box (finish the caption, then the next caption, then the next scene), Esc skips it, P
pauses. **Replay it** any time from the pause menu ("Watch Hoot's masking movie", Day 2) or the
title's **Hoot's Movies** button (also under Options). It counts as watched once it reaches the
plateau scene; skip it earlier and it plays again next visit. A 12-second recap card plays when the
masking phase starts.

You test her right ear by air (Hoot coaches the first beep and runs the rest), watch Hoot's montage
of her normal left ear, then **bone, better ear first**: the oscillator goes on her LEFT mastoid
and you test her bone there, unmasked (`>` marks). Unmasked bone is heard by the better cochlea
wherever the buzzer sits, so this is both ears' bone baseline. Hoot then moves it to her right
mastoid for ONE unmasked tone, and her left cochlea answers for the right ear: the Cross-Hearing
Ghost. Her right air is 10 dB or more poorer than that unmasked bone, so the right bone must be
masked: noise in the left ear, the plateau dance for bone (`[` marks) and then for air, and the
referral stamp to the ear doctor (a conductive loss with normal bone). About 15 minutes. Every day is open from the start: pick one
on the title's **Days** card, in any order. The suggested day wears an **UP NEXT** ribbon, and after
Day 1 The Daily Decibel stamps UP NEXT! and **Next: Day 2** goes straight on. Progress is saved.

**Day 3** brings Mike Lenders, a forklift driver who says he can't hear a thing since a forklift
beeped at work (yet he hears Hoot's whispered offer of coffee). It opens with **Hoot's spondee
movie** ("The Spondee Ladder"), an 80-second cartoon in eight scenes: why test words after beeps,
two-part words with both halves equally strong, the SRT as the halfway level, the down 10, up 5
ladder (one common way, not the only one), saying the words first by ear, and whether words and
beeps agree. Replay it from the pause menu ("Watch Hoot's spondee movie") or **Hoot's Movies**.
Hoot runs his beeps, then you find his speech recognition threshold (SRT) on the new SPEECH
screen, better ear first: go through the two-part words first, present them softer and softer,
score what Mike actually says, and find the softest level where he repeats half of them. His words come back far better than his beeps, so you present the
clues on the OBJECTION! board, never accuse him, and stamp a recheck with objective tests, with
his doctor told about the sudden loss.

**Day 4** brings Nora Nightingale, a choir director whose left ear "has gone like a radio between
stations": she hears the voices, not the words. It opens with **Hoot's word movie** ("The Letter
Choir"), a 70-second cartoon in eight scenes: why loud-enough words can still come out mushy, the
speech banana, the high-pitch curtain that hides s, f and th, words loud enough for every sound,
loud words crossing the head to the good ear, and 25 words at 4% each. Replay it from the pause
menu ("Watch Hoot's word movie") or **Hoot's Movies**. Hoot runs her beeps at top speed, then you
score her **word recognition** in the SPEECH screen's new WORD mode: one-syllable words, loud
enough for every sound, scored whole (a rhyme or a plural is a miss). Word list 1 on her right is
coached, with **NORA-VISION** showing which sounds of each word reach her. Word list 2 on her left
is yours: set the level and decide about noise in her right ear. "Is that her best?" A list 20 dB
louder makes her worse, not better: rollover. Then the **Red-Flag Roll Call** sorts nine clues,
and you stamp a referral to an ear doctor, soon, with a letter that says what you found, never
what it is. About 11 minutes.

**Day 5** brings Marco Polenta, a food critic who hears fine one-to-one and gets lost at a busy
table. It opens with **Hoot's party movie** ("The Party Problem"), a 71-second cartoon in eight
scenes: why a crowd makes the same voice harder to follow, SNR (how much louder the voice is than
the noise), why turning everything up doesn't help, SNR loss, the six courses of the **Party Noise
Test** and the receipt that adds it up (replay it from the pause menu, "Watch Hoot's party movie",
or **Hoot's Movies**). Hoot runs his beeps, bone, SRTs and words in quiet at top
speed, then you score party sentences in the SPEECH screen's new PARTY mode: one talker, a party
that gets louder every sentence (SNR 25 down to 0 dB), and five key words to tick exactly as Marco
said them. Party list 1 is coached, with a practice sentence; then you work out the bill (SNR loss =
25.5 minus the key words right), point to its band on the ruler, and set Party list 2 up yourself.
Serve the clues to Marco's two claims, test his hearing aids in the sound field in a blind tasting
(programme A, then B), see whether directional microphones really help, and seat him for dinner.
About 12 minutes.

**Days 6 to 13 are the case files**: one patient and one puzzle each, about 8 or 9 minutes. You
gather the clues (two rounds of history questions with the red-flag check every time, a look in
both ears, the **tympanometer**, the beeps, and bone and masking wherever the chart needs them),
name the shape or the type of loss, then stamp the call: **FIT** hearing aids, **REFER** to a doctor
first, or **DOCTOR TODAY**, and prove it with the one clue that decides it. Hoot's three case
movies ("Three Stamps and the Red Flags", "The Eardrum Trampoline", "Slopes, Notches & Cookie
Bites") play just before the steps they explain. Day 6 is Rocco Rimshot, a drummer with ringing
ears, who picks his own earplugs; Day 7 Captain Marina Gale, a ferry captain whose spinning spells
come with a low-pitch loss in one ear; Day 8 Dash Delgado, whose right ear went yesterday morning,
with an hours clock ticking in the corner; Day 9 Ginger Snapp, a ventriloquist whose puppet heckles
while you read her nine-year-old chart; Day 10 Barnaby Bumble, a beekeeper with a wall of wax, who
comes back a week later with the plug in a jar for a retest; Day 11 Skye Tailwind, cabin crew with
fluid behind the eardrum before Friday's flight; Day 12 Morgan Gale, the lighthouse keeper, whose
deaf ear "answers" until the noise goes in (the shadow curve); and Day 13 Bev Bingham, a bingo
caller with gaps in both ears that run Hoot out of masking room until he lends his insert earphones
(with a Hoot movie on the masking dilemma just before).
The game never diagnoses: Hoot says what a pattern points to, and a doctor decides.

**Day 14, Gus's Fitting**, is the finale: Gus comes back for his hearing aids. You pick the style
(a tiny speaker with an open dome, because his low pitches are nearly normal), slide the probe tube
in until it sits near his eardrum, then match his prescription on the real-ear graph band by band
while the grey world colours in and Doris's "the finches are back" comes through. Doris drops a pan
to check that loud sounds are loud but never painful, Gus tries to dodge the dishes, and you tell
him what to expect in his first weeks. About 8 minutes.

**Day 15, Gus's Tune-Up**, is his first follow-up, two weeks later. You ask where he has trouble
(an open question gets the stories), read the aids' diary of hours worn, then fix each complaint
at the tune-up desk with the right tool: soften the loud highs for the sharp teaspoons, run the
feedback test for the whistle at hugs, switch on a noisy-places program for the bingo hall, and
talk through why he should wear them all day. Before that, a listening tube finds the plugged wax
guard that made his right aid go quiet, and after it you plan his next visits. Then it's bingo night
at Bev's hall. Hoot's movie "The Tune-Up" plays first. About 8 minutes.

**Freestyle** (on the title) is the walk-in clinic: eight patients in the waiting room, any order,
as often as you like, or **Surprise me**. There is no script and no coaching during the tones: you
pick the tools yourself (questions, the otoscope, the tympanometer, air, bone and masking), name
the type of loss in each ear, and make the call (fit, refer, doctor today, or no aids needed). Hoot
debriefs at the end.

**Arcade** (on the title) is the quick-play corner, a minute or two a run with a high-score table:
**Chart Chute** (drop each audiogram down the chute of its shape: slope, notch, cookie bite,
rising or reverse bite), **Ears, Please!** (stamp each patient file at the clinic's front desk FIT,
REFER or DOCTOR TODAY, and point at the red flag behind every referral), **Air & Bone** (each
chart shows air and bone: call the loss sensorineural, conductive or mixed), **Tymp Toss** (sort
tympanograms into types A, B, As, C and Ad) and **Degree Drop** (sort charts mild to profound by
the 500-2k average). None ever tests your own hearing.

> **This is a game, not a hearing test.** Every "dB" in it is simulated. The beeps are kept quiet
> on purpose (about -71 dBFS at 0 dB HL, never above -12 dBFS, with a limiter on top), and you
> never need to hear them: every beep lights up on screen and every answer from Gus does too.
> The "too loud" gags are visual only; the game never plays a loud sound.
> Worried about your own hearing? Please see a licensed hearing professional.

## Run it (Windows)

1. Copy `BoothCamp.exe` anywhere and double-click it. Nothing to install; the game is one file.
2. Windows SmartScreen may say "Windows protected your PC" because the file is not code-signed.
   Click **More info**, then **Run anyway**.
3. Needs 64-bit Windows 10 or 11 and a graphics driver with OpenGL 3.3 (any PC from the last
   ten years). Settings and progress are saved in `%APPDATA%\Godot\app_userdata\Booth Camp`.

F11 or Alt+Enter toggles fullscreen. Esc or P pauses (volume, key style, text speed, comfort
options). Closing the window saves and quits.

### The career-fair demo

For a booth: double-click `BoothCamp-Demo.cmd` next to the exe (or start `BoothCamp.exe -- --demo`;
on the web page, add `?demo` to the address). The title is one **Play** button, and the visit is
Grandpa Gus in about seven minutes: one question, the sesame seed, the earphones, a few beeps,
the chart, then his hearing aids matched until Doris's "dishes" comes through. The paper's
**Next visitor** button wipes the visitor for the next one (its paper has no Decibucks or shop);
a visitor who walks off is reset after two minutes. With nobody at the title for 30 seconds, Hoot
and Gus play an attract loop until someone presses a key or clicks. The demo keeps its own save, so
it never touches anyone's game. `docs/DEMO.md` has the details.

## How to play

### Keyboard (default layout)

| Key | AudiOtron 3000 |
|---|---|
| **Space** (hold 1 to 2 s) | Play the beep. The ring on the TONE pad turns green at 1 s. |
| **Up** / **Down** | Louder / softer, 5 dB per tap |
| **Left** / **Right** | Lower / higher pitch (frequency) |
| **S** or **Enter** | Store the threshold (stamp it on the audiogram) |
| **N** | No response (only after a miss at the loudest level) |
| **R** / **L** | Right / left ear |
| **H** | Show or hide the key hints next to every control |
| **B** / **A** (Day 2) | Route the tone to the BONE oscillator / back to AIR (earphones) |
| **M** (Day 2) | Masking NOISE on / off (it plays in the other ear; its lamp glows while on) |
| **PgUp** / **PgDn**, or **Shift+Up** / **Shift+Down** (Day 2) | Noise louder / softer, 5 dB per tap |
| **Esc** or **P** | Pause and options |

| Key | SPEECH screen (Day 3) |
|---|---|
| **Space** | Present the next word (the TALK lamp lights while it plays) |
| **Up** / **Down** | Louder / softer, 5 dB per tap |
| **Right**, **+** or **=** | He repeated it: CORRECT |
| **Left** or **-** | He missed it (silence, half the word, or the wrong word): MISSED |
| **S** or **Enter** | Store the SRT |
| **M** | Speech noise in the other ear on / off |
| **H** | Show or hide the key hints |

| Key | SPEECH screen, word lists (Day 4) |
|---|---|
| **Space** | Present the next word |
| **Up** / **Down** | Louder / softer, before the list's first word (one level per list) |
| **Right**, **+** or **=** | She repeated the whole word: REPEATED (also "She'll get it" in Call it, and RED FLAG on the Roll Call) |
| **Left** or **-** | She missed it (a rhyme, a plural, another word or silence): MISSED (also "She'll miss it", and FINE) |
| **M** | Speech noise in the other ear on / off, before the list's first word |
| **V** | NORA-VISION: which sounds of the word reach her ear |
| **H** | Show or hide the key hints |

There is no STORE on a word list: it scores itself after word 25.

| Key | SPEECH screen, party sentences (Day 5) |
|---|---|
| **Space** | Present the next sentence (the party starts, then the talker) |
| **Up** / **Down** | Talker louder / softer, before the list's first sentence (unaided lists; Hoot sets the aided level) |
| **Right**, **+** or **=** | He got the highlighted key word: GOT IT |
| **Left** or **-** | He missed it: MISSED |
| **1** to **5** | Flip that key word |
| **G** | ALL GOT: tick all five |
| **S** or **Enter** | LOCK IN the sentence (unticked key words count as missed) |
| **M** | Hoot says why there is nothing to mask (the same mix reaches both ears) |
| **H** | Show or hide the key hints |

Prefer Up = softer? Click **Up = LOUDER** on the AudiOtron (or use the pause menu) to swap the
two arrows. Everywhere else: Space or Enter continues dialogue, Up / Down or 1 to 4 picks an
answer, and every screen says which keys it uses.

### Mouse only

Everything works with the mouse. Hold the big **TONE** pad to beep, right-click it for no
response, click **SOFTER**, **LOUDER**, the **Hz** arrows and **STORE**, or scroll over the dB
readout. Click the audiogram or a test-plan token to jump to that frequency. The **II** button
pauses. In the story, click to continue, click an answer, drag the earphone cups onto Gus's ears
and drag the evidence onto the case file. On Day 2, click the **AIR | BONE** switch and **NOISE**, the
noise arrows, drag the bone oscillator onto Bonnie's mastoid, and click the referral stamp. On
the title's **Days** card, click a day; the mouse wheel scrolls the grid when there are more days
than fit.
On Day 3, click a word card to drop it, click **PRESENT**, **CORRECT**, **MISSED**, **LOUDER**,
**SOFTER**, **STORE** and **MASK** on the SPEECH screen, and drag a clue onto Mike's claim.
On Day 4, click **PRESENT**, **REPEATED**, **MISSED**, **LOUDER**, **SOFTER**, **NOISE** and
**NORA-VISION** on the SPEECH screen and the two Call it buttons, then **RED FLAG** or **FINE** for
each Roll Call card and the stamp.
On Day 5, click **PRESENT**, **LOUDER**, **SOFTER**, a key-word chip (a click flips it, a right-click
marks it missed), **GOT IT**, **MISSED**, **ALL GOT** and **LOCK IN** on the SPEECH screen, then a band
on the bill's ruler, a clue onto a plate (drag, or click then click), the A / B switch and a seat.

### The AudiOtron in one breath

The **dance card** under the test plan always shows the rule: **heard = down 10, missed = up 5,
heard twice on the way up = STORE**. Hoot coaches you step by step on the first frequency of the
right ear, then you lead. Each threshold is a golf hole: par is the number of beeps a textbook
search usually needs, combos build while every step is by the book, and the scoring is fair:
when Gus wobbles it never costs you a bogey, and a slip never earns a birdie.

On the second ear you test a few frequencies on your own, then Hoot takes a turn and finishes
the ear at 8x speed so you can watch the whole routine (turn this off with **Quick visit (Hoot
runs the repeats)** in the pause menu to test every frequency yourself; Hoot's stamps never count
for or against you). On Day 2 the air test is a repeat of Day 1: her left ear, the better one, goes first as a quick
stamp montage, then Hoot coaches one beep on her right and runs the rest; the bone and masking
sheets are yours. With Quick visit off you test everything.

On Day 2 the AudiOtron grows a **MASKING** deck. The **AIR | BONE** switch shows where the tone
goes (AIR reads INSERT with the insert earphones from the shop). **NOISE** turns the masker on in
the other ear, with a lamp and a NOISE box that light whenever it plays. Hoot walks you through
the first masked bone threshold one press at a time: the starting noise (the other ear's air
+ 10, plus a few dB for a covered ear at low pitches), then "heard = noise up 5, missed = tone up
5" until the answer survives three noise raises in a row: the plateau, drawn as a flat line on
the chart. If one more noise step makes her answer vanish, the tone was crossing to the other
ear: that was the Ghost.

## What the level teaches

- **Case history:** ask open questions that find clues (which sounds are hard, how long, which
  ear), and always ask about **red flags** (pain, drainage, dizziness, sudden or one-sided change),
  any of which means a doctor checks the ears first.
- **Otoscopy:** look before you test. Gus has a sesame seed in his ear canal: never poke at it; a
  doctor or trained clinician removes it safely. (He sneezes it out, which is cartoon physics.)
- **Instructions:** "press as soon as you hear a beep, in either ear, even a faint one; let go when it stops" gives honest answers.
- **Earphones:** glasses off, red on the right ear, blue on the left (his right is your left).
- **Pure-tone audiometry (Hughson-Westlake, simplified):** start at 1000 Hz around 40 dB HL; climb
  10 dB until the first response; then down 10 after every "yes" and up 5 after every miss; the
  threshold is the lowest level heard on at least two ascending runs. Order: 1k, 1.5k, 2k, 3k,
  4k, 6k, 8k, retest 1k on the first ear (within 5 dB is clinic-grade; the better one counts), then
  500 and 250 Hz, plus 750 whenever 500 Hz and 1 kHz differ by 20 dB or more. Beeps 1 to 2 s, varied
  gaps (a steady rhythm makes patients guess), never start loud.
- **Reading the audiogram:** lower on the chart means a louder sound was needed. Gus has a gradual,
  sloping high-frequency loss in both ears: low pitches near normal, high pitches moderately
  severe. Soft consonants like s, sh, f and th live up in those high pitches, so "dishes" turns
  into a guess.
- **Fitting teaser:** hearing aids add a boost where his hearing slopes; real fittings take
  fine-tuning and a few weeks of getting used to.

At the end, The Daily Decibel scores accuracy, technique, efficiency and care, shows your golf
card and the 1 kHz retest, and pays Decibucks you can spend on a comfier booth chair (Day 2's
paper sells insert earphones).

### Day 2: what it teaches

- **Bone conduction:** the oscillator goes on the mastoid bone behind the ear, never touching the
  ear itself. Bone skips the ear canal and middle ear, so it measures the cochlea directly (tested
  at 500 to 4000 Hz; the bone oscillator tops out lower than earphones). An optional **bonus
  hole** tests bone at 250 Hz too: a loud low buzz there can be felt rather than heard, which is
  why many clinics skip it.
- **The air-bone gap:** air poorer than bone by more than 10 dB means a conductive loss. A gap of
  15 dB or more at 500, 1000 and 2000 Hz is a red flag for a referral.
- **Cross-hearing:** bone crosses the skull with about 0 dB of loss, so unmasked bone is heard by
  the better cochlea, wherever the oscillator sits: that is why bone goes on the better ear first.
  Air crosses too once the tone is at least 40 dB above the other ear's bone (on-ear phones; real
  heads lose 40 to 80 dB, so 40 is the safe rule).
- **When to mask bone:** the test ear's air 10 dB or more poorer than the unmasked bone (ASHA 2005,
  BSA 2018). Hoot says it with her numbers ("Right air 50, unmasked bone 5: a 45 dB gap"). A
  gap of exactly 10 is masked but still names no conductive loss: that needs more than 10.
- **Masking (simplified):** the noise goes in the OTHER (non-test) ear. Start it at that ear's air
  threshold + 10, plus the occlusion effect for bone at low pitches (a covered ear hears bone
  louder); heard = noise up 5, missed = tone up 5; the tone heard at the same level through three
  noise raises (15 dB) is the plateau. Too much noise crosses back (overmasking). Then masked air:
  on-ear phones lose at least 40 dB crossing the head, so her right air minus 40 could still reach
  her left cochlea (its BONE threshold), and air gets masked too. With the insert earphones (at
  least 55 dB lost) it cannot, and no masked air is needed. The masking dilemma is only mentioned.
- **The takeaway:** a conductive loss with normal bone means an ENT referral. Bonnie's pattern
  is the classic picture of otosclerosis: extra bone grows around the stapes (the stirrup bone),
  so it cannot vibrate freely. Her bone line has a small dip at 2 kHz, the **Carhart notch**: a
  stiff middle ear fakes a small bone loss there, and her cochlea is fine. The ENT confirms it;
  then an operation that swaps the stapes for a tiny piston, or hearing aids, both of which can work well. The game never
  diagnoses.
- **The Day 2 board:** the air-bone gap drawn as a river, with the bone line as the bridge over
  it and a toll booth: the "toll" is the dB the middle ear charges before sound reaches the
  cochlea.

Symbols follow the usual audiogram conventions: O / X for air, < / > for unmasked bone,
[ / ] for masked bone, triangle / square for masked air; bone symbols are often left unjoined
(some clinics join them with a dashed line).
The masking noise is synthesized narrowband noise, quiet and capped like the beeps, and it
always lights the NOISE box and the booth squiggles, so Day 2 is playable without sound too.

### Day 3: what it teaches

- **Spondees:** two-part words said with equal stress on both parts (rainbow, doorbell), chosen
  because they are about equally easy to hear. Go through them first at a comfortable level, and
  drop any word the patient doesn't know.
- **The SRT (speech recognition threshold):** better ear first; start about 15 dB above the pure-tone
  average (PTA: the average of the beeps at 500, 1000 and 2000 Hz; up to 40 above is fine too);
  repeated = down 10, missed = up 5 (one common way, not the only one), and another word at the
  same level is fine; the SRT is the softest level where at least two words and half of them come
  back, with fewer than half right 5 dB below it. Score only what the patient said: half a word is
  a miss.
- **PTA/SRT agreement:** a reliable SRT lands within about 6 dB of the PTA (12 is fair). The
  direction matters: Mike's SRT is about 40 dB better than his beeps, a classic sign of a
  non-organic loss; an SRT worse than the PTA points to the instructions, language or word
  recognition instead.
- **Never accuse:** non-organic loss has many causes (money, stress, worry, even a real loss
  underneath), and we can't tell which from the booth. Gather the clues (the agreement, the
  whispered coffee, clear canals), stay kind, recheck, refer for objective tests, and tell his
  doctor about the sudden loss. The game never calls Mike a liar.
- **Word recognition (Day 4):** play one-syllable words loud enough for every sound (the game's rule:
  the PTA plus 35, never below 55), score whole words only, 25 words at 4% each, and keep one level
  and one set-up per list. Loud words can cross the head, so speech noise goes in the other ear when
  the level minus 40 (with earphones) reaches that ear's best bone. A poor score earns one more list
  20 dB louder; a score that falls when louder is rollover.
- **Red flags in word scores:** a best score below what the tones predict, two ears too far apart
  for chance, or rollover, with a one-sided loss, a one-sided hiss or unsteadiness, means an ear
  doctor should look further: refer soon, with imaging asked for in the letter, before any hearing
  aid. A sudden change would mean a doctor today. The game never names a diagnosis.
- **Speech in noise (Day 5):** an audiogram can't tell you how someone copes in noise. The Party
  Noise Test scores key words (exactly as said; little words never count) in sentences that get
  noisier, and SNR loss = 25.5 minus the key words right, two lists averaged. Near-normal, mild,
  moderate or severe points to the technology that helps (good habits, standard settings,
  directional microphones, a remote microphone). Aided tests go in the sound field at a chatting
  level, a difference between programmes counts only when it beats the critical difference, and
  the booth is kinder than a real restaurant.

The words are an original list written for the game, shown on screen as you play: no list, order
or recording is copied, and every word is checked against ASHA (1988) Appendices A-C and CID W-1.
Each word plays as a quiet murmur through the same safety limits as the beeps; a recording can
replace it later, and nothing depends on hearing it. Day 4's "Word list 1" to "Word list 4" were
written fresh for the game the same way: everyday words, each with rhymes that change only its key
sound, and no published word list, order or rhyme set copied. Day 5's practice sentence and
"Party list 1" to "Party list 6" were written fresh for the game too: everyday sentences with
five key words each, and no published sentence test's sentences, lists, order or recordings copied.

## Build from source

Requires [Godot 4.7.2](https://godotengine.org) (standard build, GDScript only) and, for the
Windows build, the 4.7.2 export templates. All art, sound and music are made in code at runtime;
the only asset files are the Fredoka font (SIL Open Font License, `assets/fonts/OFL-Fredoka.txt`).

```bash
godot --headless --path . --import                  # first time, or after adding files
tools/run_tests.sh                                  # unit tests (exit 0 = green)
tools/autoplay_check.sh                             # a bot plays the whole level headless
tools/autoplay_check.sh --day=2                     # ...Day 2 (bone and masking)
tools/autoplay_check.sh --day=2 --upgrades=insert_earphones   # ...Day 2 with inserts
tools/autoplay_check.sh --via-days --pick=bonnie   # ...picking Day 2 on the Days card
tools/autoplay_check.sh --day=3                     # ...Day 3 (speech, SRT)
tools/autoplay_check.sh --day=4                     # ...Day 4 (word recognition)
tools/autoplay_check.sh --day=5                     # ...Day 5 (speech in noise)
tools/autoplay_check.sh --day=6                     # ...Day 6, a case file (also 7 to 13)
tools/autoplay_check.sh --day=14                    # ...Day 14 (the fitting)
tools/autoplay_check.sh --day=15                    # ...Day 15 (the tune-up)
tools/autoplay_check.sh --demo                      # ...the career-fair demo
tools/mouse_check.sh                                # the same with mouse clicks only
godot --path .                                      # play it

mkdir -p build
godot --headless --path . --export-release "Windows Desktop" build/BoothCamp.exe
```

`tools/README.md` lists the other checks: rendered screenshots of a full playthrough
(`tools/shots.sh`), the audio-safety recording that measures every tone
(`tools/movie_check.sh`), and a button-mashing stress test of the AudiOtron
(`--scene=res://tools/monkey.tscn`). `CONTRACTS.md` describes how the modules talk to each other.
The designs are in `docs/`: `CHAPTER2.md` to `CHAPTER5.md` and `SPEECH.md` (Days 2 to 5), `CASES.md`
(Days 6 to 13), `FITTING.md` (Day 14), `TUNEUP.md` (Day 15), `FREESTYLE.md`, `ARCADE.md` and `DEMO.md`.
